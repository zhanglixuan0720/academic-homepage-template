## A wearable navigation system for blind and visually impaired


Blind and visually impaired people face significant challenges when navigating in unfamiliar outdoor environments. The outdoor environment faces obstacles, traffic signals, and societal norms. These factors often hinder the mobility of the blind and visually impaired and make long-distance navigation particularly arduous.
Current navigation systems fall short in these situations: SLAM methods can navigate accurately but are limited to smaller areas, while GPS devices can cover long distances but suffer from significant location errors.
In this work, we propose a proof-of-concept wearable navigation system named eLabrador to assist blind and visually impaired individuals in navigating long distances in unfamiliar outdoor environments. This wearable system is designed to leverage public maps (e.g., Amaps or Google Maps) and GPS devices for long-distance navigation while utilizing computational visual perception for precise and safe local guidance. This combination accurately guides blind individuals to walk over long distances in unfamiliar outdoor environments. 

Specifically, this system adopts a head-mounted RGBD camera to perceive typical objects and areas in outdoor traffic environments, aggregating them as a 3D semantic map as the fine-grained perception of the surrounding environment. Afterward, the planning module generates a precise walking path, utilizing the 3D semantic map and the route prior from the global Amap. Finally, the interaction module exploits the audio-haptic hybrid feedback to convey navigation information to blind and visually impaired people.  The overall three modules work together to navigate blind and visually impaired people walking long distances in outdoor environments.

The presented system is evaluated on two real-world scenarios of unfamiliar outdoor traffic roads for 10 blind and blind-folded subjects. The experiments show that our system can successfully and safely navigate blind and visually impaired while walking outdoors. Furthermore, our system offers descriptive feedback on landmarks and navigation cues, aiding blind and visually impaired users in comprehending their surroundings. Additionally, our subjective assessments reveal that a majority of the visually impaired participants expressed a favorable disposition towards our system, noting a feeling of safety and an acceptable cognitive load.
